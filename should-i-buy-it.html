Purchase decision guide

Should I buy it?

A good purchase is not just one you can afford today. It should make sense for how often you will use it, what it will cost over time, and what you expect to get back from owning it.

Start with expected use

A £300 purchase used 300 times costs roughly £1 per use before upkeep. The same item used 15 times costs £20 per use. Neither figure automatically makes the purchase good or bad, but the difference is large enough to change the decision.

Estimate use conservatively. Think about what your routine looks like after the novelty wears off, not only what you intend to do in the first week.

Ask what problem the purchase solves

Some purchases replace something broken, remove a genuine inconvenience or save meaningful time. Others are mainly about enjoyment. Both can be legitimate reasons to spend money, but they should not be treated as the same type of decision.

A useful question: if this product disappeared from sale tomorrow, would you still feel that a real problem in your life needed solving?

Check the full cost of ownership

Include predictable extra costs where they matter: replacement parts, maintenance, subscriptions tied to the product, consumables or accessories you know you will need. Then compare that total with the number of uses you genuinely expect.

Delay purchases driven mainly by urgency

Limited-time offers, social proof and the feeling that you need to decide immediately can make a weak purchase feel stronger than it is. If the product is not solving an urgent need, giving yourself a day before buying can reveal whether the desire lasts.

Use a consistent method

TruWorth combines price, extra costs, expected use, enjoyment, ownership effort, time saved and your reason for wanting the product into a consistent 0–100 score. The point is not to make the decision for you; it is to make the trade-offs visible before money leaves your account.
